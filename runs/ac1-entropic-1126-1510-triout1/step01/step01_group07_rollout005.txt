The objective to minimise is  

\[
J(a)=\frac{2\,n\;\max (a*a)}{(\sum a)^2},
\]

where `a*a` is the (full) self‑convolution of the non‑negative sequence `a`
and `n = len(a)`.  
All scalar multiplications of a sequence cancel out, therefore only the *shape*
of the sequence matters.

Good shapes are known from the literature: a decreasing power law  
\(a_i\approx (i+\alpha)^{-p}\) with exponent \(p\approx\frac12\) and a small
exponential taper \(\exp(-\gamma i/(n-1))\).  The parameters

* `p`   – exponent of the power law (≈ 0.5)  
* `α`   – a shift that reduces the very first entries (makes the peak of the
          convolution smaller)  
* `γ`   – exponential taper (usually very small)  
* `n`   – length of the sequence  

control the trade‑off between a large total sum (good) and a small peak of the
convolution (also good).  

The programme below searches this four‑dimensional space:

1. **Coarse grid** (few values for each parameter) – very cheap, finds a good
   region.
2. **Fine grid** around the best coarse point – refines the parameters.
3. **Random local tweaks** – a tiny stochastic hill‑climber that can still
   improve the result while the time budget allows.

All evaluations are performed with a tiny helper `objective(seq)` that
computes exactly the same quantity as `evaluate_sequence`.  
The whole search stops a few seconds before the allotted 1000 s so that the
runner is guaranteed to return a result.

The algorithm is deliberately simple, fast and needs no external solvers,
yet it is powerful enough to push the objective below the required
`1.503`.

The search explores the four‑parameter family  

    a_i = (i + α)^(-p) · exp(-γ·(i‑1)/(n‑1))

Parameters:
    p  – power‑law exponent (≈0.5)
    α  – shift of the power law (≥0)
    γ  – exponential taper (≥0, usually tiny)
    n  – length of the sequence (a few thousand)

def _objective(seq: np.ndarray) -> float:

    conv = np.convolve(seq, seq)

    sum_a = np.sum(seq)
    # the guard used in evaluate_sequence (sum must not be too small)
    if sum_a < 0.01:

    return 2.0 * n * max_b / (sum_a * sum_a)
