The evaluation function can be rewritten in terms of the *probability vector*  

\[
p_i=\frac{a_i}{\sum_j a_j},\qquad \sum_i p_i = 1 ,
\]

because the whole expression is homogeneous of degree 0:

\[
\frac{2n\,\max_k (a*a)_k}{(\sum a)^2}
   =2n\;\max_k\bigl(p*p\bigr)_k .
\]

Thus we have to minimise the maximum entry of the **linear** convolution
\(c=p*p\) of a non‑negative probability vector.
For a uniform vector \(p_i=1/n\) the convolution is a triangle and the
maximum equals \(1/n\); the resulting score is exactly 2.
If we make the entries follow a decreasing power law  

\[
p_i\propto (i+1)^{-\alpha}\qquad (\alpha>0) ,
\]

the centre of the convolution is much flatter and the peak becomes
roughly  

\[
\max_k(p*p)_k\;\approx\; \frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{n}\,
\Beta(1-\alpha,1-\alpha) .
\]

Choosing \(\alpha\approx0.6\) gives a constant factor ≈ 0.68, i.e. a
score around 1.35 – well below the target 1.503.  
The computation is cheap: building the sequence, normalising it and
evaluating it with an FFT‑based convolution takes only a few
milliseconds even for several thousand entries.

The algorithm therefore

1. picks a length `n` (taken from the previous construction if
   available, otherwise a modest default),
2. scans a small grid of exponents `α∈[0.4,0.8]`,
3. builds the corresponding power‑law sequences,
4. evaluates them and keeps the best,
5. optionally performs a tiny local search (mass transfer between two
   positions) while time permits.

All numbers are non‑negative, lie inside the `[0,1000]` interval and the
total sum stays far above the 0.01 safety bound, so the evaluator never
returns `inf`.
